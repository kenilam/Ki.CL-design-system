Accessibility starts with how you think about disability. It's often shortened to a11y, for the eleven letters between the first _a_ and the last _y_.

## A mismatch, not a condition

Since 2001 the World Health Organization has described disability as an interaction between a person's health and their environment, not only as something a person has. For a wheelchair user, the barrier is the stairs, not the wheelchair. Someone who can't see a screen is only shut out by an interface that has to be seen.

That moves the problem to where we can do something about it. We can't change someone's body, but we can change the page. Every time an interface assumes its user can see, hear, use a mouse, read quickly or hold still, it creates a mismatch for someone.

## Permanent, temporary and situational

Disabilities differ in kind, in how severe they are and in how long they last. Kat Holmes and Microsoft's inclusive design team describe the last one as a spectrum: permanent, temporary and situational. For each kind, in that order:

- **Moving:** one arm, a broken wrist, or holding a child.
- **Seeing:** blind, dilated pupils, or bright sunlight.
- **Hearing:** deaf, an ear infection, or a loud train.
- **Thinking:** dementia, concussion, or constant interruptions.
- **Speaking:** non-verbal, laryngitis, or speaking a second language.

Designing for the permanent case often helps with the temporary and situational ones too. A page that works with one hand works for the parent holding a child, and captions written for deaf viewers help everyone on a loud train.

## Solving the edges improves the middle

Some everyday technologies grew out of accessibility work. Talking books were recorded for blind readers, many of them soldiers blinded in the First World War. The multi-touch gestures on today's touchscreens came from FingerWorks, whose co-founder had repetitive strain injury and needed a gentler way to type. When the hard cases work, the common case usually gets better too.

On the web, the same idea is why this system starts from native elements. A real `button` already works with a keyboard, a screen reader, voice control and a switch device. A `div` with a click handler works with a mouse, and every other way in has to be rebuilt by hand.
